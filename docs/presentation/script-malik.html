Presenter script

Malik — reservation logic, optimization, business value

Your speaking time: about 8 minutes across the talk, plus Q&A lead. You open the presentation and you close it.

Slides 1–2 · Opening and team 1:00 · you start

"Good morning. We are Malik, Abdel Aziz and Aya, and we built ChargeHub — a reservation system for electric vehicle charging stations."

"Before we start, one thing worth saying: everything you will see today runs on real code and a real database. There are no mock-ups and no screenshots pretending to be a working system. When we click something, it really happens."

"I worked on the booking rules and the optimization engine. Abdel Aziz built the back end, the database and the operator side. Aya built the customer journey and the dashboards."

Transition → "Let me start with the problem, because the problem is what makes this more than a booking form."

Slides 3–4 · Introduction and the problem 2:00

"A charging station is a shop with very few seats. Charging a car takes anywhere from fifteen minutes to two hours, and a station might only have three or four chargers. So if a driver arrives and everything is busy, that driver is simply lost."

Then walk the four problems on slide 4, one sentence each:

"First — two drivers can book the same charger at the same moment, and one of them drives there for nothing. Second — a driver finishes early or cancels, that time is free again, but nobody knows, so it is wasted. Third — people who never turn up block a charger somebody else wanted. Fourth — staff end up deciding all of this by hand, under pressure."

"So our goal was simple to say and hard to do: serve the most drivers possible, be fair about it, and never sell the same charger twice."

Hand over → "Abdel Aziz will show you how it is built."

Slides 10–12 · Business logic — your main section 4:00 · your strongest material

Slide 10 How the optimizer decides

"A driver tells us 'about thirty minutes, any time between two and six'. The system looks at every possible slot across every compatible charger and gives each one a score."

"Five things go into that score. Does this keep the free time in usable blocks, rather than leaving unbookable gaps. How close is it to the time they actually wanted. Have they been waiting a long time already. Are they standing at the desk, or are they being rebooked after a fault that was ours. And how likely are they to actually turn up."

Say this clearly: "This is not artificial intelligence. It is a fixed set of rules with fixed weights. The same situation always gives the same answer, and we can always say why. If a customer asks why they were given four-thirty instead of three, there is a stored sentence explaining it."

"And we do not just claim it helps. Every time it runs, it also calculates what plain first-come-first-served would have achieved on exactly the same data. If our system does not serve more people, the number tells us."

Slide 11 The five-minute hold and reliability

"When we offer a driver a charger, we genuinely hold it for five minutes. Nobody else can book it in that window. That is what makes accepting safe — the charger was already secured when we made the offer, so pressing accept cannot fail."

"It is five minutes whether the booking is fifteen minutes or two hours. That was a deliberate decision. If the hold scaled with the booking length, then the most valuable bookings would be the most expensive ones to offer."

"And if you answer late? You do not get an error. The system runs again and offers you a new time. Being slow should not throw you out of the queue."

Point at the four scores: "Reliability starts at one hundred for everyone. Turning up adds a point. Arriving late, cancelling late, or not turning up takes points away. These four are real drivers from our demo data — same system, very different behaviour."

Important, say it: "A low score never stops anyone booking. It only decides who gets the better slot when two people want the same one. We were careful about that — a system that could lock people out is a ban dressed up as an optimisation."

Slide 12 Deposits and fairness

"A small deposit holds the booking. Cancel more than a day ahead and you get it all back. Cancel at the last minute, or never turn up, and it is kept."

The line that matters: "But our fault always beats the clock. If the charger broke, or we moved your booking, you are refunded even inside the twenty-four hour window — and your reliability score is not touched. Only staff can mark something as our fault. If a driver could do that, they would refund themselves every single time."

Say this before anyone asks: "Payments are simulated. No card details are accepted, stored or shown anywhere in this system. The records and the refund rules are real; the money movement is not."
Hand over → "Aya is going to show you all of this actually working."

Slide 15 · Business value 1:00 · after the demo

"So what is this worth to a station owner?"

"More drivers served, because freed time is offered to someone waiting within a minute instead of sitting empty. Fewer wasted trips, because the database makes double booking impossible. Fewer no-shows, because the deposit gives a reason to turn up — and cancelling early is always free, so it rewards telling us. Less staff work, because the system decides and explains, and staff step in only when they choose to."

"And decisions based on evidence. A manager can see which station is short of capacity and why people are waiting there, rather than guessing."

Slides 16, 18, 19 · Future work, lessons, close 2:00

Future work — be honest and brief: "Real card payments; the connection point is already built, so it is a swap rather than a rebuild. Email and text alerts — messages work inside the app today. And a real check-out signal: right now we assume a driver leaves when charging stops, and a sensor would tell us for certain."

Lesson six (slide 18), your closer: "The lesson that surprised us most was that testing the logic is not the same as testing the wiring. At one point every single one of our one hundred and eighty-two automatic checks was passing — while half of our dashboard showed no data at all. The logic was correct. Nothing had ever fed it. Now we write a check that would fail if two correct pieces disagreed with each other, and that habit found four real problems that reading the code had missed."

Closing: "ChargeHub turns a charging station from first-come-first-served into a system that plans — and can always explain its decision. Nothing you saw is a mock-up. Nothing can double-book. And nothing happens without a recorded reason. Thank you — we are happy to take questions."

Q&A: You lead. Route database and implementation questions to Abdel Aziz, interface and analytics questions to Aya. Use the Q&A Handbook — you have 50 answers prepared. If you genuinely do not know, say "we did not build that" rather than guessing; the handbook lists what is deliberately not built.
