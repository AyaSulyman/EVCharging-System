Slides 4 and 6 · Introduction and the problem 2:00

"A charging station is a shop with very few seats. Charging a car takes anywhere from fifteen minutes to two hours, and a station might only have three or four chargers. So if a driver arrives and everything is busy, that driver is simply lost."

Then walk the four problems on slide 4, one sentence each:

"First — two drivers can book the same charger at the same moment, and one of them drives there for nothing. Second — a driver finishes early or cancels, that time is free again, but nobody knows, so it is wasted. Third — people who never turn up block a charger somebody else wanted. Fourth — staff end up deciding all of this by hand, under pressure."

"So our goal was simple to say and hard to do: serve the most drivers possible, be fair about it, and never sell the same charger twice."

Slides 17–18 · Business logic — your main section 3:10 · your strongest material

Slide 17 How the optimizer decides

"A driver tells us 'about thirty minutes, any time between two and six'. The system looks at every possible slot across every compatible charger and gives each one a score."

"Five things go into that score. Does this keep the free time in usable blocks, rather than leaving unbookable gaps. How close is it to the time they actually wanted. Have they been waiting a long time already. Are they standing at the desk, or are they being rebooked after a fault that was ours. And how likely are they to actually turn up."

Say this clearly: "This is not artificial intelligence. It is a fixed set of rules with fixed weights. The same situation always gives the same answer, and we can always say why. If a customer asks why they were given four-thirty instead of three, there is a stored sentence explaining it."

"And we do not just claim it helps. Every time it runs, it also calculates what plain first-come-first-served would have achieved on exactly the same data. If our system does not serve more people, the number tells us."

Slide 18 The five-minute hold and reliability

"When we offer a driver a charger, we genuinely hold it for five minutes. Nobody else can book it in that window. That is what makes accepting safe — the charger was already secured when we made the offer, so pressing accept cannot fail."

"It is five minutes whether the booking is fifteen minutes or two hours. That was a deliberate decision. If the hold scaled with the booking length, then the most valuable bookings would be the most expensive ones to offer."

"And if you answer late? You do not get an error. The system runs again and offers you a new time. Being slow should not throw you out of the queue."

Point at the four scores: "Reliability starts at one hundred for everyone. Turning up adds a point. Arriving late, cancelling late, or not turning up takes points away. These four are real drivers from our demo data — same system, very different behaviour."

Important, say it: "A low score never stops anyone booking. It only decides who gets the better slot when two people want the same one. We were careful about that — a system that could lock people out is a ban dressed up as an optimisation."

Worth saying why it is a slide and not a video: showing the three-offer cap live would take fifteen minutes of waiting.

Slides 8–9 · Architecture 2:15 · Malik hands to you

Slide 8 Two applications, one database

"Thank you Malik. The system is two applications sharing one database."

"The part people see is built with Next.js and React — the driver's app, the operator's app and the manager's dashboards. Behind it is a back end with fifty-three endpoints, and that is where every business rule lives. The screens never touch the database directly. That matters because it means a rule cannot be enforced in one screen and forgotten in another."

"Underneath is MongoDB with fourteen collections. And one thing worth pointing out — we keep a history of everything that happens to a booking, and that history is only ever added to. It is never edited. Scores and reports are calculated from it rather than stored."

Slide 9 The one rule the database enforces — your key slide

"This is the most important technical decision we made, so I want to spend a moment on it."

"We split charger time into fifteen-minute blocks. A booking from three to four o'clock owns four of those blocks. And we told the database: one block can be owned by only one booking, ever."

Point at the diagram: "So when Ben tries to book three-thirty to four-thirty, he needs two blocks Anna already owns. The database refuses it. Not our code — the database itself. But when Ben books four o'clock to five, nobody owns those blocks, so it works. Two bookings sit back to back with no time wasted between them."

Say why: "We could have written this check in our own code. We started that way. The problem is that a check in code can be skipped by a feature somebody writes later, and if two requests arrive at exactly the same moment they can both pass it. A rule inside the database cannot be bypassed by code that does not exist yet."

If a mentor pushes: MongoDB has no built-in way to say "these two time ranges must not overlap". Splitting into fixed blocks turns overlap into a simple duplicate-key rule, which the database does enforce natively. The cost is that bookings start on the quarter hour. We accepted that trade deliberately.

Slide 10 · Built to survive its own failures 0:40 · new — cannot be filmed

Why this slide exists: none of these four can appear in any video. You would have to crash the system, corrupt a projection or send a duplicate message on purpose to show them. Say that out loud — it is the point of the slide, not an excuse.

"Four decisions you will not see in any of our videos, because each one only shows itself when something goes wrong."

Take two of the four, not all: "We write the booking first and claim the time second. So if the system dies half way, we are left with a booking holding nothing — which our repair tool finds and reports. The other order would leave time held by nothing: invisible to every query, bookable by nobody, and impossible to notice. We chose which way it breaks."

"And every message carries a key the database refuses to duplicate. Run the message worker twice and you get zero new messages, not two copies of everything."

The closing line: "None of that is demonstrable without deliberately breaking our own system — which is exactly why it is worth telling you about."

Slide 29 · Design lessons 1:15

"Three lessons from the design side."

"First — preventing double bookings belongs in the database, not in our code. We tried it in the application first. A check in code can be skipped by a feature written later, and two requests arriving together can both pass it. Moving it into a database rule made it impossible to break."

"Second — we had to model charger time as small blocks. We started with fixed thirty-minute slots, but real charging is anywhere from fifteen minutes to two hours. Splitting time into fifteen-minute blocks let us support any length with one simple rule. And we got the boundary wrong once, in a way that made every back-to-back booking impossible — which taught us to test the edges, not the middle."

"Third — scores have to be rebuilt from history, never counted up as you go. A running total drifts. If one update is missed or repeated, the number is wrong forever and nobody can tell. Recalculating from the recorded history means a repeated event cannot double-count and a lost one repairs itself."

Hand over → "Malik has the three that surprised us most."

Questions likely to come to you Q&A

QuestionShort answer
Why MongoDB and not SQL?Honest answer: it is what the team knew. A relational database with an exclusion constraint would express the overlap rule more directly. We got the same guarantee by splitting time into blocks and using a unique index.
Why fifteen minutes?It divides evenly into every length we support — 15, 30, 45, 60, 90 and 120. No rounding and no wasted time. One minute would multiply the number of database writes by fifteen for precision nothing needs.
What if two people click at exactly the same millisecond?One of them gets a duplicate-key error from the database and is told the charger is busy. This is tested — it is one of our automatic checks.
How do you know the station scoping works?We tested it by running it: the assigned station is allowed, an unassigned one is refused with a 403, and an admin bypasses it by design. We can show that.
Is the QR code secure?It contains only the booking code, which the operator could also type. It is a lookup shortcut, not a credential — and the operator still has to be logged in and assigned to that station.
Are the payments real?No. They are simulated, and no card data of any kind is accepted, stored or displayed. What is real is the rules and the records — every deposit, refund and forfeiture is written down with its reason.
How hard would real payments be?The connection point already exists, along with the payment-intent and refund records and the protection against charging twice. It is a swap, not a rebuild.
Could a driver claim it was your fault to get a refund?No. Only staff or an admin can set the operator-fault flag, and that is checked on the server.

Why this is yours: rendering is really a question about how the page feels to the person using it — how fast it appears and whether the information is right the moment they see it. That is the customer journey, which is your half of the project.

Slide 12 Three ways to build a page

"Thank you Abdel Aziz. Next.js gives us three different ways to build a page, and we chose per page rather than picking one for the whole project."

"Server-rendered means the server builds the page fresh for every visitor. Static means it is built once when we deploy and then reused for everybody. And browser-rendered means a light shell arrives first, and then the browser fetches the data."

"Each one is right in a different situation, so the interesting part is not the three names — it is why we picked each one."

Slide 13 Every page, and why

"The public pages — the home page, the station list, a station's detail page — are server-rendered. A driver looking for a charger, and a search engine indexing us, both need to see real availability in the very first response. If those pages were cached, we would be showing yesterday's answer."

The line to land — say it slowly: "The QR landing page is server-rendered and never cached at all. Showing a bay as free when it was taken thirty seconds ago is the one failure this whole product exists to prevent."

"The information pages — about, FAQ, terms, privacy — are static. They are identical for everyone and change very rarely, so building them once at deploy time is simply faster for the visitor."

"And the driver's own area — the booking wizard, their bookings, their offers, the waitlist — is browser-rendered. Those pages need the login token, and they involve a lot of clicking. A static shell appears instantly and the personal data arrives a moment later, which feels much faster than waiting for a full page."

Be honest, it scores well: "We also configured a time-based refresh option, but it is currently unused — every request asks for fresh data. That is on our list to tidy up."

Questions likely to come to you Q&A

"Did you design this yourself?" — Yes. One set of colours, fonts and components used across every screen, with a minimum touch size so it works on a phone.

"Does it work on mobile?" — Yes, every screen is built for small screens first.

"Why does that say 'no data'?" — Because there genuinely is none for that period. We deliberately never show zero in place of missing data — a zero looks like a real measurement and would be misleading.

"What is the countdown on the offer?" — The charger is really held for five minutes. The number comes from the server, not the phone's clock, so a device with the wrong time cannot show a hold that has already expired as still valid.

"Can I see the deposit figures?" — Be honest: the data is all recorded, but we have not built a screen for it yet. It is on our list.
